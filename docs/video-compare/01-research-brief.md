# Video compare feature: research brief

Verified facts gathered by research agents (web + codebase) before designing. Confidence in brackets.

## youtube

Bottom line: the literal request, a stick figure drawn on top of a YouTube video, can't be built in a policy-compliant way. Getting the pose data out of a YouTube video is either impossible from page code (it can't read the cross-origin iframe), against YouTube's terms (downloading with yt-dlp), or both very risky and desktop-Chrome/Edge-only (tab capture).

What each route turned out to be:
- **Server-side yt-dlp download.** Breaks the YouTube Terms of Service and the API Developer Policies III.E.1. In 2026 it is also unreliable on datacenter IPs like Render's. Bot checks ("Sign in to confirm you're not a bot") are common there and need a logged-in account's cookies, and yt-dlp warns that the account can get banned. It now also needs PO tokens and Deno/EJS. And it would break the app's "nothing leaves your device" promise.
- **Reading the embedded player's pixels in the browser.** Not possible. canvas.drawImage does not accept an iframe, and the same-origin policy only allows postMessage with a cross-origin iframe.
- **Tab capture (getDisplayMedia with preferCurrentTab, CropTarget or RestrictionTarget).** Technically works, but only on desktop Chromium. It is not supported on Android Chrome, iOS Safari or Firefox for Android, and preferCurrentTab and the cropping APIs are Chrome/Edge only. It needs a consent prompt every time the user shares the tab. Policy-wise it collides with Developer Policies III.I.14 (no technology other than the API to access any part of the video), III.E.4 (no "new or derived data" from API Data, which explicitly includes audiovisual content) and III.I.16 (no derivative works).

What the policies allow:
- **The YouTube player itself must not be touched.** The Required Minimum Functionality rules forbid "overlays, frames, or other visual elements in front of any part of a YouTube embedded player", even a transparent canvas. They also forbid changing the player beyond what the API documents, so no CSS mirroring. The player must be at least 200x200 px; 480x270 is recommended.
- **Anything beside or below the player is allowed**, including a separate synced canvas. The IFrame API is good enough for rough sync only, not frame-accurate sync (details in the findings).
- **Embedding brings duties:**
  - Ads may play and cannot be blocked.
  - Some videos refuse to embed (errors 101/150), and age-restricted videos don't play on other sites.
  - The privacy policy and terms must mention YouTube.
  - Made-for-Kids status has to be checked with the Data API.
  - The /privacy page's claims "Zero Third-Party Tracking" and "Nothing leaves your device" would need rewording.

Recommended design:
1. **Analysis needs a video file.** The reference is either the teacher's own recording or a creator's own video downloaded from YouTube Studio (720p/360p, up to 5 downloads per video per day). Process it on the device with PoseLandmarker; the baking pipeline from the deleted /mocap page (commit 287aba3) can be recovered. The skeleton can then be drawn freely on our own video element, because that is our player and not YouTube's.
2. **A YouTube link becomes an optional "watch beside" companion.** Show it unmodified on youtube-nocookie.com, click-to-load, with playsinline=1 and controls on, next to the student's view. Feedback for this mode either needs no reference (mudra classifier, posture rules) or comes from an uploaded file.
3. **Don't build the server download or the tab-capture path.** Both have their legal and practical blockers.

Because YouTube time readings are imprecise and ads or buffering can interrupt playback, any comparison should tolerate timing offsets (for example a DTW-style alignment) rather than match frame to frame.

1. **[high] Drawing a skeleton or any canvas on top of an embedded YouTube player is explicitly forbidden, even if it is transparent or click-through. Changing the player's look (for example a CSS mirror, scale or filter) is also forbidden.**
   - Evidence: Required Minimum Functionality (last updated 2026-09-14), section 'Overlays and frames': "You must not display overlays, frames, or other visual elements in front of any part of a YouTube embedded player, including player controls. Similarly, you must not use overlays, frames or other visual elements to obscure any part of an embedded player, including player controls." Same page, 'YouTube Player attributes': "You must not make changes to the YouTube player that are not explicitly described by the API documentation." Size rule: "Embedded players must have a viewport that is at least 200px by 200px. If the player displays controls, it must be large enough to fully display the controls ... We recommend 16:9 players be at least 480 pixels wide and 270 pixels tall." Developer Policies III.I.4: "you must not remove, obscure, alter, or disable any links that appear in YouTube players". III.I.6: "modify, build upon, or block any portion or functionality of a YouTube player". III.I.7: "separate, isolate, or modify the audio or video components of any YouTube audiovisual content". Nothing in these pages restricts elements placed beside or below the player.
   - Sources: https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/required-minimum-functionality ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies
   - Design implication: Never absolutely position a canvas over the YouTube iframe, even with pointer-events:none, and never mirror or flip it with CSS. The compliant layout is side by side on desktop and stacked on mobile: an unmodified player with controls on (at least 480x270 recommended, never below 200x200) and our own skeleton canvas or student video in a separate box next to or below it. A mirror mode can only apply to our own canvas or the student's video.
2. **[high] Downloading a YouTube video on the server (yt-dlp or similar) to run pose estimation breaks both the YouTube Terms of Service and the API Developer Policies.**
   - Evidence: YouTube ToS (effective Dec 15, 2023), Permissions and Restrictions: "You may view or listen to Content for your personal, non-commercial use. You may also show YouTube videos through the embeddable YouTube player." You are not allowed to: "access, reproduce, download, distribute, transmit, broadcast, display, sell, license, alter, modify or otherwise use any part of the Service or any Content except: (a) as expressly authorized by the Service; or (b) with prior written permission from YouTube and, if applicable, the respective rights holders"; "access the Service using any automated means (such as robots, botnets or scrapers) except ... with YouTube's prior written permission"; "circumvent, disable ... or otherwise interfere with ... features that (a) prevent or restrict the copying or other use of Content". Developer Policies III.E.1: "You and your API Clients must not, and must not encourage, enable, or require others to: download, import, backup, cache, or store copies of YouTube audiovisual content without YouTube's prior written approval, make content available for offline playback ..."
   - Sources: https://www.youtube.com/static?template=terms ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies
   - Design implication: Do not add a yt-dlp or 'paste a link and we fetch it' backend. It is a ToS violation regardless of whether it works technically. It would also send video through a server, which contradicts the product's on-device privacy promise.
3. **[high] Even setting the ToS aside, yt-dlp from datacenter IPs (Render, AWS, GCP, Hetzner) is unreliable in 2025-2026. Bot checks that need cookies are common there, using an account risks a ban, PO tokens are being enforced, and a JavaScript runtime (Deno) plus the EJS solver scripts are now required.**
   - Evidence: bgutil PO-token provider README (the PO Token Guide says it is maintained by a yt-dlp maintainer): "Providing a PO token does not guarantee bypassing 403 errors or bot checks" and "a PO Token does not bypass IP-based login restrictions. If you still get 'Sign in to confirm you're not a bot' after a token was generated (common on datacenter IPs), you additionally need to pass cookies." yt-dlp Extractors wiki: "YouTube is gradually enforcing the use of a 'PO Token' to be able to download videos" and "By using your account with yt-dlp, you run the risk of it being banned (temporarily or permanently)." yt-dlp README: yt-dlp-ejs is "Required for full YouTube support" and "A JavaScript runtime/engine like deno (recommended) ... is also required". EJS wiki: "To download from YouTube, yt-dlp needs to solve JavaScript challenges presented by YouTube using an external JavaScript runtime." Issue #17376 (Aug 6, 2026, yt-dlp nightly 2026.08.04 + bgutil 1.3.1): LOGIN_REQUIRED / intermittent 403, reporter notes "Public VPN and datacenter IPs fail more frequently". The tracker shows about 149 issues matching "confirm you're not a bot", with recent ones from March to October 2026. Guest rate limit per the wiki: about 300 videos/hour.
   - Sources: https://github.com/Brainicism/bgutil-ytdlp-pot-provider ; https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/Extractors ; https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/EJS ; https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/PO-Token-Guide ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/master/README.md ; https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/issues/17376 ; https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/issues?q=is%3Aissue+%22confirm+you%27re+not+a+bot%22+sort%3Acreated-desc
   - Design implication: Running this on the Render free tier (512 MB, sleeps when idle) would also mean installing ffmpeg, Deno, the EJS scripts and a PO-token server, and it would still break in production as soon as Render's IP is flagged. The fix would be logged-in cookies, which risks a ban and is even more clearly against the ToS. Rule this path out completely.
4. **[high] A web page cannot read the pixels of a cross-origin YouTube iframe, so MediaPipe cannot be fed the embedded player's frames directly.**
   - Evidence: MDN drawImage: the image argument may be an HTMLImageElement, SVGImageElement, HTMLVideoElement, HTMLCanvasElement, ImageBitmap, OffscreenCanvas or VideoFrame. An iframe is not an accepted source. MDN Same-origin policy: "When two documents do not have the same origin, these references [iframe.contentWindow ...] provide very limited access to Window and Location objects ... To communicate between documents from different origins, use window.postMessage." So the <video> inside youtube.com's iframe cannot be reached. MDN on tainted canvases: drawing cross-origin data without CORS makes getImageData/toBlob/captureStream throw SecurityError. YouTube's own widget API (www-widgetapi.js) talks to the player only through postMessage ("infoDelivery" / "initialDelivery" messages).
   - Sources: https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/CanvasRenderingContext2D/drawImage ; https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Security/Defenses/Same-origin_policy ; https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/How_to/CORS_enabled_image ; https://www.youtube.com/iframe_api
   - Design implication: No canvas, WebGL or MediaPipe pipeline can sample frames from the embed. The IFrame API returns only state, time, rate and metadata, never pixels. Any YouTube-based skeleton would need a different source of frames, and the remaining technical option is tab capture (next findings).
5. **[high] Tab self-capture can technically grab the embedded player's rendered pixels, but only on desktop Chrome and Edge, and only after the user picks the current tab in a share prompt every session. Region Capture (CropTarget) and Element Capture (RestrictionTarget) do not exist on any mobile browser, Firefox or Safari.**
   - Evidence: MDN browser-compat-data: getDisplayMedia is in Chrome 72, Edge 79, Firefox 66 and Safari 13 on desktop, but chrome_android, firefox_android and safari_ios are all false. preferCurrentTab: Chrome 94 only (Firefox and Safari false). CropTarget/cropTo: Chrome 104, desktop only. RestrictionTarget/restrictTo: Chrome 132, desktop only. Chrome docs: "Region Capture is available from Chrome 104 on desktop only"; with Region Capture "Pixels occluding the target will be captured"; Element Capture "lets you capture and record a specific HTML element", available in Chrome 132 on desktop, and removes occluding content. The Element Capture explainer says it can be invoked "only if the user shares the (entire) current tab" after the usual getDisplayMedia consent, and notes the target "might have sub-elements that can be navigated, like iframes". The target must form a stacking context (e.g. isolation: isolate). A getDisplayMedia stream is a normal MediaStream that could go into the same video-to-MediaPipe path CameraFeed already uses for camera streams. That this stream doesn't taint the canvas is my inference, not a documented guarantee.
   - Sources: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/api/MediaDevices.json ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/api/CropTarget.json ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/api/RestrictionTarget.json ; https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/main/api/BrowserCaptureMediaStreamTrack.json ; https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/region-capture ; https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/element-capture ; https://github.com/screen-share/element-capture
   - Design implication: Even if it were allowed, tab capture would exclude every phone user (likely most students, which is an assumption about this audience), Firefox and Safari. It also adds a screen-share prompt plus Chrome's 'sharing this tab' bar every session. It cannot be the main path, at most a desktop-Chromium experiment, and the next finding argues against shipping it at all.
6. **[medium] Capturing the YouTube player's pixels to extract pose landmarks conflicts with several clauses of the YouTube API policies, even if done on the device and never stored. The wording is broad enough that this is a real compliance risk, not just a gray area.**
   - Evidence: Definition of 'API Data': "data, content (including audiovisual content) and information provided to API Clients ... through the YouTube API services". So the video played in an embed counts as API Data. Developer Policies III.I.14: you must not "use any technology other than YouTube API Services to access or retrieve API Data, including to access any portion of any YouTube audiovisual content". III.E.4 (Refreshing, Storing, and Displaying API Data): "Your API Clients must not (i) replace API Data with similar, independently calculated data, or (ii) access or use API Data to create new or derived data or metrics." III.I.16: must not "modify, translate, create derivative works of ... any YouTube API Services", which by definition include API Data. III.I.7: must not "separate, isolate, or modify the audio or video components". III.E.1: must not "cache, or store copies". All of these apply to what you do and to what you "encourage, enable, or require others to" do. ToS: no access to Content "except ... as expressly authorized by the Service". The only exception named is YouTube's prior written approval. New section III.L (from June 1, 2026) allows derived metrics only for "audited developers with analytics use cases that have explicitly applied for permission".
   - Sources: https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/api-services-terms-of-service ; https://www.youtube.com/static?template=terms
   - Design implication: Do not ship 'process the YouTube link' through tab capture. Saving landmark 'bakes' made from a YouTube video in IndexedDB would make it worse (derived data plus storage). If the team wants the YouTube link input anyway, frame it honestly as 'watch beside': no skeleton drawn from YouTube content, and analysis needs an uploaded file. Confidence is medium only because YouTube has not issued a specific ruling on ephemeral on-device analysis; the clause wording itself is unambiguous.
7. **[high] The IFrame Player API can sync a separate canvas or video only roughly. getCurrentTime is a cached value that the client library extrapolates, seeks snap to keyframes, rate changes are only suggestions, and there are no per-frame callbacks.**
   - Evidence: Docs: getCurrentTime() "Returns the elapsed time in seconds since the video started playing." The current www-widgetapi.js (player build 1b3be681) implements it as `k=this.playerInfo.currentTime; if(playerState===1){f=(Date.now()/1E3 - this.playerInfo.currentTimeLastUpdated_)*this.playbackRate; f>0&&(k+=Math.min(f,1))}`. In other words it returns the last value the player sent over postMessage, extrapolated by wall-clock time and capped at 1 s ahead. Precision therefore depends on postMessage delivery, not on video frames. seekTo(seconds, allowSeekAhead): "The player will advance to the closest keyframe before that time unless the player has already downloaded the portion of the video". If paused it stays paused, otherwise it plays. The 'start' parameter docs say the keyframe offset is "usually no more than around two seconds". setPlaybackRate: "Calling this function does not guarantee that the playback rate will actually change". Unsupported values are rounded "down to the nearest supported value in the direction of 1". Use getAvailablePlaybackRates() (e.g. 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2) and react to onPlaybackRateChange. Loading or cueing a video resets the rate to 1. Events: onReady, onStateChange (-1, 0, 1, 2, 3, 5), onPlaybackQualityChange, onPlaybackRateChange, onError (2, 5, 100, 101, 150), onApiChange, onAutoplayBlocked. Parameters: controls=0/1; playsinline=1 is needed for inline play on iOS (the default 0 means fullscreen); modestbranding is deprecated with no effect; rel=0 only limits related videos to the same channel; loop needs playlist=VIDEO_ID; enablejsapi=1 plus origin should be set. Autoplay counts as scripted playback, may be blocked by the browser (onAutoplayBlocked), and "A playback only counts toward a video's official view count if it is initiated via a native play button".
   - Sources: https://developers.google.com/youtube/iframe_api_reference ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/player_parameters ; https://www.youtube.com/s/player/1b3be681/www-widgetapi.vflset/www-widgetapi.js
   - Design implication: For any side canvas synced to YouTube:
- Read getCurrentTime() in a requestAnimationFrame loop and accept tens to hundreds of milliseconds of jitter. Do not attempt frame-exact comparison against YouTube time.
- After seekTo(t, true), wait for onStateChange (PLAYING or PAUSED) and re-read the time instead of assuming the seek landed exactly.
- Implement step loops with your own end-time check and seekTo. Offer slow motion only from getAvailablePlaybackRates().
- Keep controls=1 and playsinline=1, and use no autoplay (a click-to-play facade).
- Comparison logic must tolerate timing offsets (DTW or windowed alignment) instead of matching index to index.
8. **[high] Embeds may show ads that we cannot block. Owners can disable embedding. Age-restricted videos won't play on third-party sites.**
   - Evidence: YouTube Help, 'Ads on embedded videos': "Embedded videos may show skippable or non-skippable in-stream ads." and "Only YouTube and the video owner will earn revenue from ads on embedded videos. The site owner where the video is embedded will not earn a share." Developer Policies III.I.5: must not "modify, interfere with, replace, or block advertisements placed or served by YouTube". Embed help: "Age-restricted videos can't be watched on most 3rd party websites. These videos will redirect viewers back to YouTube when played." Owners can uncheck 'Allow embedding'. IFrame API onError: "101 – The owner of the requested video does not allow it to be played in embedded players. 150 – This error is the same as 101." Embedders must send an HTTP Referer, otherwise viewers get "error 153". NrityaVaani's next.config.ts sets no Referrer-Policy or CSP today, so browser defaults would send the origin.
   - Sources: https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/132596?hl=en ; https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/171780?hl=en ; https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6301625?hl=en ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/iframe_api_reference ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies
   - Design implication: The sync loop must treat 'not PLAYING' or 'time not advancing' as unsynced, since ads and buffering can interrupt. On errors 100, 101, 150 or 153, show a friendly message such as 'This video can't be embedded; upload the file instead'. Don't add a strict no-referrer policy later, or embeds will break with error 153.
9. **[high] Embedding user-supplied YouTube links comes with privacy and legal duties. Today's /privacy page claims ('Zero Third-Party Tracking', 'Nothing leaves your device') would no longer be accurate.**
   - Evidence: Developer Policies III.A: "API Clients must display a link to YouTube's Terms of Service ... and they must also state in their own terms of use that, by using those API Clients, users are agreeing to be bound by the YouTube Terms of Service." That lookup is Data API videos.list with part=status, checking status.madeForKids, and needs an API key and quota. Privacy-enhanced mode: use www.youtube-nocookie.com; ads served there are non-personalized. Repo: frontend/src/app/privacy/page.tsx says "Zero Third-Party Tracking" and "Nothing leaves your device".
   - Sources: https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/guides/made_for_kids_status ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos ; https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/171780?hl=en

10. **[high] The compliant way to get an analyzable reference is a video file the user has rights to: their own recording, or a creator downloading their own video from YouTube Studio. YouTube offers no API that exposes frames or movement data for other people's videos.**
   - Evidence: YouTube Help 'Download your own YouTube videos': creators can download "MP4 files of YouTube videos that you've uploaded to YouTube in either 720p or 360p". "You can only download an individual video up to five times per day". Videos with a copyright or Community Guidelines strike, or using pre-approved audio tracks, can't be downloaded. "You can also use Google Takeout to download all the videos you've uploaded." "You can't download other user's YouTube videos." The ToS's only other route is "prior written permission from YouTube and, if applicable, the respective rights holders."
   - Sources: https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/56100?hl=en ; https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos ; https://www.youtube.com/static?template=terms ; https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2797468?hl=en
   - Design implication: Make 'Upload reference video' the primary input:

- Clean the tracks with despikeTrack, smoothTrack and stabiliseTrack from frontend/src/components/three/retarget.ts.
- Store bakes in IndexedDB.
- Draw the stick figure on top of our own <video> element, which is allowed because it is not a YouTube player.
- In the UI, tell teachers and creators they can get a file of their own YouTube video from YouTube Studio > Content > Download.
- Keep the YouTube link only as an optional reference player shown beside the analysis, with reference-free tips for the student (mudra classifier, posture and aramandi rules) or comparison against an uploaded file. Never extract a skeleton from YouTube itself.

## browser-vision

Short answer: the on-device plan is workable, but only if the browser processes the video ahead of time rather than live. Use PoseLandmarker plus a HandLandmarker run on a magnified crop around each wrist; this is the pipeline the deleted /mocap page already had. Don't use HolisticLandmarker. Read frames with WebCodecs (through Mediabunny or mp4box.js) and fall back to seeking the video element where WebCodecs is missing. Store the landmarks as packed Float32Array data in IndexedDB. Running the models live while the video plays is too slow, and the existing code already found this.

Hard constraints the plan has to design around:
1. **No pixels from a YouTube embed.** The iframe is cross-origin, and the IFrame API only gives time, seek and playback rate. Downloading breaks YouTube's Terms of Service, and yt-dlp is blocked from datacenter IPs such as Render's. The only way to keep processing on-device is to capture the current tab (getDisplayMedia + Region Capture). That works only in desktop Chrome, only while the video plays in real time, and asks permission every time.
2. **Hands at full-body distance.** The palm detector was trained on hands closer than about 2 m, and a hand in full-body 720p video is only about 65 to 140 px long. Mudra feedback should be best-effort and given only while the hand is held still.
3. **Accuracy limits.** Depth (z) is only up to scale. Side views swap left and right legs. Loose clothing such as a saree, and fast motion blur, hurt accuracy. The model card covers one person only: detection gets worse beyond about 4 m, and a missing head is out of scope.
4. **Timestamps in VIDEO mode.** They must strictly increase, or the call fails with "Packet timestamp mismatch". They also drive the built-in One-Euro smoothing, so pass the real frame times.
5. **Mirroring can't be seen in the pixels.** Whether a phone video is mirrored depends on the phone and its settings. Hand handedness assumes a selfie (mirrored) image.

Speed: about 10 to 30 ms per frame for pose on a laptop GPU (full or heavy model), and about 40 to 125 ms on an older mid-range Android phone. The repo's own heavy pose + 2 hand crops measured about 95 ms per frame; a 2-minute clip at 24 fps took roughly 10 minutes. Sampling at 10 to 15 fps, using the full model on phones and heavy on desktop, keeps a 1-minute student clip to about 1 to 2 minutes of processing on a phone.

2. **[high] VIDEO vs IMAGE mode work differently inside. VIDEO mode tracks the person from the previous frame's landmarks and skips the detector, and applies One-Euro temporal smoothing (only when numPoses == 1). IMAGE mode runs the detector on every frame, with no tracking and no smoothing.**
   - Evidence: pose_landmarker_graph.cc: 'While in stream mode, skip pose detector graph when we successfully track the poses from the last frame' (PreviousLoopbackCalculator + DisallowIf). Not in stream mode: 'Always use the pose detector graph.' Lines 303-311: smooth_landmarks = use_stream_mode only if num_poses == 1, else error 'Currently pose landmarks smoothing only supports a single pose.' pose_landmarks_detector_graph.cc: visibility low-pass (alpha 0.1) plus a OneEuro-style filter (min_cutoff 0.05, beta 80; world: 0.1/40). landmarks_smoothing_calculator_utils.cc: OneEuroFilter.Apply(timestamp, ...) uses the packet timestamps. In the local bundle, IMAGE mode calls hc(t,e,n,t.C+1), an internal auto-incrementing timestamp. GitHub #5253 shows VIDEO mode giving different results for identical frames because of this state.
   - Sources: 
   - Design implication: For whole-clip processing, use VIDEO mode with one sequential pass per clip, so you get tracking (faster) and built-in smoothing. Use IMAGE mode only for one-off random-access re-detection, such as a scrubbed frame or a retry. Keep the existing despikeTrack/smoothTrack from retarget.ts as a centred offline pass after detection; don't double-smooth heavily.

## algorithms

This is research only. I changed no repo files. I ran one throwaway benchmark script in the scratchpad (/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-NrityaVaani/9f010986-1750-5e8b-bbef-bf11e3828af6/scratchpad/bench/sdtw.mjs).

RECOMMENDED APPROACH (after several rounds of attacking my own proposals). It is semi-automatic and works at the level of steps. Everything runs on the device and is rule-based:

(1) INGEST. An uploaded reference file is processed offline in the browser. A YouTube link cannot be processed from the iframe:
- The video is cross-origin, so its pixels can't be read.
- Downloading it on the server breaks YouTube's ToS, and Render's datacenter IPs get "Sign in to confirm you're not a bot".
- The only on-device route is tab self-capture with getDisplayMedia plus Region Capture. That works on desktop Chrome/Edge only, runs in real time while the video plays, and the overlay must stay hidden during capture.
- YouTube's Required Minimum Functionality forbids drawing anything in front of the embedded player. So for YouTube the stick figure goes in a synced panel beside the player. For uploaded files it can be drawn over the video.

(2) TWO-PASS PROCESSING.
- A quick scan at about 5 fps with the lite model finds dance vs talk and proposes steps.
- The user ticks which steps they are practising. This is our answer to "not all movement is necessary". Products do the same: Just Dance and Peloton only score moves or time windows they authored or the instructor cued.
- Only the chosen steps then get a detailed bake at about 15 fps (with hand crops only if needed).
- This matters because the old mocap baker took "the better part of ten minutes" for a 2-minute clip.

(3) STRUCTURE.
- Find talking stretches with lower-body stillness for 2 s or more plus an upright posture (knees not in aramandi).
- Do not use segment.ts's 0.35 s pause default. Bharatanatyam holds a key posture on each beat for about 0.5–1 s, so that default would cut a step at every beat.
- Inside each step, detect the repetition period with a self-similarity matrix or autocorrelation, and fold the repetitions into one canonical cycle.

(4) MATCH.
- Find the student's dancing intervals.
- Estimate the tempo ratio from the ratio of the student's cycle period to the reference's (adavus are danced at 1x, 2x and 4x speed, which is beyond DTW's usual 0.5–2x slope limit). When both videos use the same music, audio onset cross-correlation can give this instead.
- Resample the student track to the reference tempo.
- Run subsequence DTW (open begin, open end, slope steps {(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)}) of each student interval against the concatenated reference steps, with talking frames masked out.
- Pick the best match per student interval, then choose matches in order with a small dynamic program.
- Unmatched student time is "not recognised, not scored". Unmatched reference steps are "not attempted", not zero. Müller & Appelt's rule applies: no alignment is better than a bad one.
- Compare each student repetition to the canonical cycle by phase. This removes the "matched the wrong repetition" problem.
- Mirroring is decided once per video: run the match on the original and on a mirrored copy (x negated, left/right swapped) and keep the clearly cheaper one. If the costs are close, offer a toggle and suppress any "wrong side" feedback.

(5) COMPARE.
- Use joint angles plus bone unit vectors from MediaPipe world landmarks, centred and scaled on the body.
- Correct only for yaw, using one camera-yaw estimate per video. Do not use full per-frame Procrustes, because it would also remove real torso lean and choreographed turns.
- Weight every comparison by the visibility of that joint in both videos.

(6) SCORE.
- Score per step and per body group (legs/aramandi, arms, torso, timing, hands).
- Ignore deviations below about 15°, because MediaPipe's knee-angle error from a front view can be ±15°. Saturate the penalty around 45°.
- Do not score a body group whose coverage is low.
- Show bands (Needs work / Getting there / Good / Excellent) rather than a precise percentage.
- Timing comes from the DTW path: its local slope gives tempo drift, and its offset at key-posture frames gives early or late.

(7) FEEDBACK.
- Use rule templates keyed by feature and direction of deviation, plus absolute Bharatanatyam checks that work even if matching fails: aramandi depth and turnout, upright torso, arms at shoulder line, left/right symmetry, regular foot strikes.
- Rank by severity × duration × importance and show the top 3.
- Phrase tips with an external focus, as motor-learning research recommends.
- The top tip can be spoken by a Guru voice. Each tip links to the moment in both videos.

COST.
- 10 min reference × 2 min student at 15 fps is 9000×1800 = 16.2M cells. Exact subsequence DTW took 2.3 s in Node on desktop with 24-D features. Backpointers need 16 MB with rolling rows.
- Coarse-to-fine (5 fps search, then a ±1 s band at 15 fps) cuts this to about 0.3 s.
- Do not use FastDTW.
- HMMs need several recordings per step to train. With one reference they reduce to DTW, so skip them for v1.

1. **[high] No single temporal-alignment method covers all six cases. Plain global DTW handles tempo changes (a) but fails on partial clips (b), talking sections (c), skips and extras (e) and repeats (d). Subsequence (open-begin/open-end) DTW adds (b) and (f), and (c) if talking frames are masked. Partial-match or jump variants are needed for (e) and for repeated structure (d). Cross-correlation handles only a constant offset. HMMs need training data.**
   - Evidence: FMP notes on subsequence DTW: the first row of the accumulated cost matrix is set to the local cost, so a match may start anywhere; the end is the minimum of the last row; several matches are the local minima of the length-normalised matching function with an exclusion neighbourhood; complexity is O(NM). Müller & Appelt (ICASSP 2008): 'In classical DTW, all elements of one sequence are matched to elements in the other sequence ... problematic when elements in one sequence do not have suitable counterparts'. They add that 'subsequence DTW or partial matching strategies ... do not properly account for such structural differences', and that repeated parts show up as 'striped regions' giving 'more or less random gaps'. Open-begin/open-end DTW needs one extra n×m matrix of start indices and stays O(nm). JumpDTW handles repeats and jumps in score following (over 99% bar-level accuracy on Beethoven sonatas), but later work notes it 'cannot skip certain parts of the score'.
   - Sources: https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/FMP/C7/C7S2_SubsequenceDTW.html ; https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2008_MuellerAppelt_PathConstraintPartialSync_ICASSP.pdf ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.2264 ; https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2010_FremereyMuellerClausen_PartialSync_ISMIR.pdf ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2105.08531
   - Design implication: Do not run one global DTW over the whole videos. Structure the matching around steps. Use subsequence DTW to place each student interval inside the reference, choose the matches in order with a small dynamic program, and allow explicit 'no match' results instead of forcing every frame to align.
2. **[high] The safest rule for skipped or added moves is 'no alignment is better than a bad alignment'. Path-constrained partial matching gets most sections right but not all, so the UI must treat unmatched material as neutral rather than wrong.**
   - Evidence: Müller & Appelt: 'In case a part in one audio recording does not have a suitable counterpart in the other recording, we prefer to have no alignments rather than having bad alignments.' They build a match from greedily extracted high-score diagonal paths, then clean it so long runs win. Their result: 87% of path components correct and 43% strongly correct at 3 s tolerance (85%/58% of matches at 5 s). That is good, but roughly 1 in 7 sections is still wrong even in a published system on music.
   - Sources: https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2008_MuellerAppelt_PathConstraintPartialSync_ICASSP.pdf
   - Design implication: Label student time that matches nothing as 'not recognised, not scored'. Label a reference step with no match as 'not attempted', not 0%. Show the aligned pair of clips so the student can see what was matched, and let them correct a wrong match (for example 'this was Step 3') with one tap.
3. **[high] Exact DTW is affordable in the browser at our sizes. FastDTW should not be used; use exact DTW with an explicit band, or a coarse-to-fine pass.**
   - Evidence: My benchmark (scratchpad/bench/sdtw.mjs, Node on desktop, one thread): subsequence DTW with slope steps {(1,1),(1,2),(2,1)}, a 24-D feature distance computed per cell, three rolling rows and a Uint8 backpointer grid. 1800×9000 (2 min student × 10 min reference at 15 fps) = 16.2M cells took 2290 ms with 16.2 MB of backpointers. 1200×6000 took 1051 ms. With 48-D features, 1800×9000 took 3250 ms. Storing a full Float32 cost matrix would need about 65 MB. Wu & Keogh: FastDTW is approximate and generally slower than exact DTW in realistic use.
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.11246
   - Design implication: Run the matcher in a Web Worker. First search at 5 fps (600×3000 = 1.8M cells, about 0.25 s), then refine at 15 fps in a ±1 s band around the coarse path (about 110k cells). Keep only rolling cost rows plus Uint8 backpointers. Expect phones to be 3–5x slower than desktop, which is still a few seconds. Matching per step shrinks the problem much further.
4. **[medium] Repeated phrases (d) are the main ambiguity, but matching the 'wrong' repetition only matters when the repetitions differ: right/left alternation, or different speeds. Folding the reference repetitions into one canonical cycle and comparing each student repetition to it by phase removes the ambiguity.**
   - Evidence: Mallick, Das & Majumdar found adavus are cyclic sequences of key postures; for example Natta 1 cycles C02→C01→C03→C01 across a 16-beat phrase, and 23 postures cover 8 Natta variants. RepNet (Dwibedi et al., CVPR 2020) uses a temporal self-similarity matrix as the bottleneck for class-agnostic period estimation. Classic motion-capture work also detects repeats from posture self-similarity. Müller & Appelt report that repeated sections produce striped similarity matrices and random alignment gaps.
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11023 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.15418 ; https://www.hubertshum.com/publications/icuimc2008repetitive/files/icuimc2008repetitive.pdf
   - Design implication: For each reference step, build a pose self-similarity matrix and find the period from diagonal-stripe or autocorrelation peaks (on device; no learned model needed for v1). DTW-average the repetitions into one canonical cycle. Map each student frame to a cycle phase, and score per repetition ('rep 3: aramandi rose 20°'). Measure features per side so that a cycle that alternates right and left is detected as one full cycle.
5. **[medium] Bharatanatyam speeds can break the usual DTW tempo limit. Adavus are practised at three speeds (each roughly double the last), so tempo ratios of 2x–4x are normal, while the standard slope steps {(2,1),(1,2),(1,1)} only allow 0.5x–2x.**
   - Evidence: FMP: step sizes {(2,1),(1,2),(1,1)} constrain the tempo ratio between matched segments. Course material (eambalam) states that students aim to perform all adavus in 3 speeds and on both sides. Unconstrained steps {(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)} allow any ratio but produce degenerate alignments.
   - Sources: https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.de/resources/MIR/FMP/C7/C7S2_SubsequenceDTW.html ; https://www.eambalam.com/?p=28496 ; https://dynamictimewarping.github.io/py-api/html/api/dtw.dtw.html
   - Design implication: Normalise tempo before matching. Estimate the ratio from the student's cycle period divided by the reference's, or from audio (beat or onset cross-correlation when the same track is playing). Resample the student track to the reference tempo, then run DTW with the 0.5–2 slope steps and a band. Report the detected speed to the student ('you danced at 2x speed').
6. **[high] Pauses and held key postures cause two problems. The current segmenter would over-segment Bharatanatyam, and DTW tends to produce singularities on plateaus where nothing moves.**
   - Evidence: Mallick et al. found 'a Bharatanatyam dancer typically assumes a (momentarily stationary) Key Posture at a beat', spanning 16–32 frames (0.5–1 s at 30 fps). Our segment.ts cuts on lower-body stillness with defaults minPause=0.35 s and still=0.008 m/frame, so a held key posture would count as a seam between steps. Keogh & Pazzani describe DTW 'singularities', where one point maps to a long run of the other series; derivative DTW (DDTW) reduces them.
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11023 ; https://www.ics.uci.edu/~pazzani/Publications/sdm01.pdf
   - Design implication: Treat a stretch as talking only when the body is still for 2 s or more AND the dancer is standing upright (knee angle above about 160°, i.e. out of aramandi), or the dancer has left the frame. Keep held key postures inside a step. Append velocity features at about 0.3 weight to the pose features (a DDTW-style mix), and add a small penalty for horizontal/vertical steps so that holds align without collapsing.
7. **[medium] Commercial products avoid fully automatic comparison of arbitrary videos. They constrain the problem with authored metadata (which moves count, and when), and some only reward participation. That is how they handle the fact that not every movement matters.**
   - Evidence: Just Dance's camera mode compares the player's movements with the on-screen coach's choreography; Ubisoft says it can 'pinpoint exactly which parts of the body are off'. In the older controller version, each move was a pre-recorded signature with a timing window. Peloton Guide's Movement Tracker gives credit 'as long as you are moving while they are moving and are doing the correct exercise' for the duration the instructor cues; it processes on the device and shows no form score. Kemtai has an authored exercise model, a repetition-counting engine and form feedback. Steezy offers a split-screen webcam mirror, looping, slow-down and front/back views, but no auto-scoring.
   - Sources: https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/game/just-dance/2026/news-updates/2UBGhF4xWMLAVVFMmTCWwn/revolutionising-motion-gaming-the-camera-controller-feature ; https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2025/10/14/just-dance-2026-camera-controller/ ; https://www.onepeloton.com/en-AU/blog/peloton-guide-machine-learning-explained ; https://upsie.com/fitness-warranty/peloton-guide-movement-tracker-improve-workouts ; https://kemtai.com ; https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11273343 ; https://steezy.co/posts/11-things-you-can-do-with-steezy-studio-that-you-cant-do-in-a-regular-class
   - Design implication: Make the step selection human-in-the-loop. The app proposes steps; the student or teacher ticks the ones that matter and can drag boundaries. Lessons we already have (in /learn) should ship their manifests' steps and cues as the authored reference. Copy Steezy's split-screen, loop and slow-down as the base experience, even before any scoring.
8. **[high] HMMs are the textbook tool for dance sequences, but they need training data from several performers. With a single teacher video they offer nothing beyond DTW.**
   - Evidence: Mallick et al. trained one HMM per Natta adavu, with as many hidden states as key postures, and reached 94.64% recognition on 8 classes. Laraba & Tilmanne (CAVW 2016) trained HMMs on high-precision mocap of an expert, needed MLLR adaptation to Kinect data, and mapped log-likelihood to a percentage (a score above 50% unlocks the next exercise). Their related work cites DTW-based teacher/student systems that give per-joint and overall scores.
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11023 ; https://orbi.umons.ac.be/bitstream/20.500.12907/41927/1/Dance performance evaluation using hidden Markov models - SL.pdf
   - Design implication: Skip HMMs in v1. The 'states = key postures' idea is still useful: run a left-to-right DTW against the canonical cycle and report results per key posture. If the team later collects several teacher recordings per adavu, an HMM or template model becomes an option.
9. **[medium] Correlation-based offset estimation cannot handle tempo drift on its own, but audio is a strong, cheap way to seed the alignment. It is especially useful when the student records while the lesson or music plays, and stamps or ankle bells give rhythm onsets directly.**
   - Evidence: Multi-camera synchronisation work uses normalised cross-correlation of audio spectrograms, fingerprint peak maps or onset envelopes to find time offsets with confidence scores. Mallick et al. used the sollukattu audio beats to place key postures (combined with motion cues, 83.49% key-frame extraction).
   - Sources: https://ccrma.stanford.edu/~njb/research/icassp2012-multicamera.pdf ; https://research.adobe.com/publication/clustering-and-synchronizing-multi-camera-video-via-landmark-cross-correlation ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11023
   - Design implication: Optional fast path: decode both audio tracks with the Web Audio API, cross-correlate their onset envelopes to get an offset and, if both are periodic, a tempo ratio; then limit DTW to a band around that line. When the audio doesn't match (low correlation peak), fall back to pose alone. Detect stamp onsets in the student's audio to measure rhythm steadiness (variation in the gaps between onsets) without any video. For YouTube, tab audio is only available through getDisplayMedia capture.
10. **[high] For comparing poses across different bodies, distances and cameras, use translation and scale normalisation with joint angles and bone unit vectors, weighted by confidence. Full per-frame Procrustes is wrong here, because it rotates away the very errors we want to catch (torso lean) and the choreography itself (turns).**
   - Evidence: Pose Trainer normalises all distances by torso length and works on vector geometry. Move Mirror crops to the bounding box, L2-normalises, and weights distances by keypoint confidence, so that 'low confidence joints have less effect'; this was 'much better' with occlusion. Pr-VIPE notes that 2D projections of one 3D pose differ greatly across views, and applies Procrustes to 3D poses to ignore rotation, translation and scale. MediaPipe worldLandmarks are 3D, in metres, centred on the hips, and carry visibility and presence. Müller's relational Boolean features (e.g. 'right foot in front of the body', 'hand above neck height') are robust to spatial variation and easy to read.
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11718 ; https://blog.tensorflow.org/2018/07/move-mirror-ai-experiment-with-pose-estimation-tensorflow-js.html ; https://research.google/blog/recognizing-pose-similarity-in-images-and-videos/ ; https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/pose_landmarker/web_js ; https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2006_MuellerRoeder_MotionTemplates_SCA.pdf
   - Design implication: Feature vector per frame:
- knee, elbow and hip angles; shoulder elevation; torso lean from vertical; knee spread relative to hip width (a turnout proxy); wrist height relative to the shoulder line;
- unit direction vectors of the limb bones in a body frame (origin at mid-hip, scaled by torso length);
- a single yaw correction per video (median hip-line yaw), not per frame.
Weight every term by min(visibility in the reference, visibility in the student). Add a few relational Booleans so that feedback can be phrased in words. Reuse stabiliseTrack and enforceSkeleton from retarget.ts to clean tracks first.
11. **[medium] MediaPipe joint-angle error is large enough that fair thresholds have to be wide (about 15° or more), and the error depends on camera angle and clothing. Aramandi knee bend can be measured from the front, but forward torso lean cannot.**
   - Evidence: A metrological study of MediaPipe knee flexion during squats, against an IMU reference, found systematic underestimation (-13.9 ± 15.1)° with 95% limits of agreement of [-43.5°, 15.5°]. A lateral (90°) view gave (-2.3 ± 6.4)°, and tight trousers were more stable (1.6 ± 3.5)°. Another comparison reports an MAE of about 5.9° for knee angles. Domain point: in aramandi the thighs turn out, so the thigh and shin lie close to the frontal plane, and a front camera sees the knee angle well; a forward lean is a sagittal-plane movement and is hard to see from the front. Practice sarees and pleated costumes probably hurt knee tracking, an inference from the clothing effect.
   - Sources: https://iris.uniecampus.it/handle/11389/91575 ; https://books.aijr.org/index.php/press/catalog/download/163/81/3126-1?inline=1
   - Design implication: Ignore deviations of 15° or less. Score linearly from 15° to about 45°, and treat anything beyond 45° as fully wrong. Compare the student to the reference as measured by the same model, never to textbook absolute angles, so the model's bias largely cancels. Ask for a front, full-body, hip-height camera, and check framing before processing: both hips and ankles visible, and the shoulder-to-torso ratio close to the reference's. If the views differ, warn the student and reduce the weight of depth-dependent terms.
12. **[medium] Mirroring should be detected automatically, once per video, by comparing alignment costs. When the step is left/right symmetric the answer can be genuinely ambiguous, and that case needs its own handling.**
   - Evidence: Teacher practice varies: some teachers face the class and reverse the steps so students can copy them. Some video platforms mirror the picture; Zoom's setting keeps the studio orientation. Phone front cameras differ in whether they save mirrored video. This is my own engineering conclusion; I found no dance paper that solves it.
   - Sources: https://4dancers.org/2009/10/teaching-tip-mirror-mirror ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.10490
   - Design implication: Mirror the student track by negating x and swapping MediaPipe left/right indices (the P constants in retarget.ts). Run the coarse match on both versions and pick mirrored only if its cost is below 0.85 times the original's. Otherwise mark the result ambiguous, default to unmirrored, and show a 'mirror' toggle. Never decide mirroring per step, or real wrong-side mistakes would be hidden. Only say 'you used the wrong side' when the decision was confident.
13. **[medium] Scores should be per step and per body part, gated by confidence, and shown as bands. A single exponential percentage can mislead students, especially when tracking is poor.**
   - Evidence: A CS231N AI dance-coach project maps DTW distance with Accuracy = e^(-d/100). The constant was tuned to the spread of distances in their own dataset, so it doesn't carry over to other data. They flag a segment above a fixed DTW threshold (10) and weight the overall score by segment duration. Laraba & Tilmanne map normalised log-likelihood through a score function and use 50% as the bar to pass. Just Dance gives discrete per-move ratings. Move Mirror shows that confidence weighting matters with occlusion.
   - Sources: https://cs231n.stanford.edu/papers/CS_231N_Final_Project__Latex_Writeup_Update - Henry Jingsong Zhou.pdf ; https://orbi.umons.ac.be/bitstream/20.500.12907/41927/1/Dance performance evaluation using hidden Markov models - SL.pdf ; https://blog.tensorflow.org/2018/07/move-mirror-ai-experiment-with-pose-estimation-tensorflow-js.html
   - Design implication: Score each step on legs/aramandi, arms, torso, timing and hands, using visibility-weighted mean angular error with the dead-band above. If a body group has under about 60% of its frames with good visibility, show 'couldn't see your feet clearly' instead of a number. Show a band label with the reason. Calibrate the band edges by scoring the teacher video against itself and against a few known-good and known-bad student takes before launch.
14. **[medium] The DTW warping path itself gives timing and rhythm errors that students can understand: its local slope is relative tempo, and its offset at key-posture frames shows whether the student is early or late.**
   - Evidence: 'Warping and Matching Subsequences Between Time Series' turns warping paths into readable shift, compression and stretching between subsequences. Bharatanatyam key postures fall on beats (Mallick et al.), so the beat frames are natural checkpoints.
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.15452 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11023
   - Design implication: After tempo normalisation, take a 1–2 s rolling slope of the path. Report 'rushing' or 'dragging' where the slope stays more than about 15% off 1 for at least one second. Report tempo consistency as the variation of that slope. Report early or late arrival at each key posture in milliseconds, and suppress values under about 100 ms, since 15 fps sampling plus tracking jitter cannot resolve less. Plot the tempo curve under the timeline.
15. **[high] Useful feedback comes from rule templates keyed by body feature and direction of deviation, ranked by severity and frequency. Only a few tips should be shown, phrased with an external focus.**
   - Evidence: SAGE-Yoga learns an acceptable angle range per joint from reference samples; below the range it suggests opening or straightening the joint, above it suggests relaxing or bending. Pose Trainer uses exercise-specific angle thresholds with fixed sentences (e.g. upper arm-torso angle range >35° means shoulder swing). In the CS231N study, feedback raised user accuracy from 55.9% to 66.8%, and 85% of feedback was judged coherent; that used an LLM, but the frame selection was rule-based. Motor-learning research: the guidance hypothesis says feedback on every trial can hurt learning; external-focus feedback works well even when frequent (Wulf et al.).
   - Sources: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.20245 ; https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11718 ; https://cs231n.stanford.edu/papers/CS_231N_Final_Project__Latex_Writeup_Update - Henry Jingsong Zhou.pdf ; https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00190/full
   - Design implication: Build a template table indexed by step, feature and direction. Examples:
- knee bend too small: 'Sit deeper into aramandi — your knees are ~25° straighter than the Guru's in Step 2'
- elbow below shoulder: 'Lift your elbows to the shoulder line'
- torso lean: 'Keep your chest upright'
- left/right asymmetry: 'Your left knee bends less than your right'
- timing: 'You rushed the second half of Step 3'
- drift across reps: 'Your aramandi rises by rep 4'
Rank by deviation × fraction of frames × joint importance. Show the top 3 plus one 'focus for next take'. Use external-focus wording ('push the floor away', 'draw a line from fingertip to fingertip'). The top tip can be read by a Guru persona through the existing TTS. Each tip links to the aligned timestamps in both videos, with red joints on the stick figures.
16. **[medium] Bharatanatyam-specific checks can be computed from the student video alone, without the reference. That gives useful feedback even when the alignment is ambiguous or fails.**
   - Evidence: Araimandi geometry: the arms, knees, torso and feet should form 'a series of three triangles', with knees bent and feet turned out making 'a diamond shape between the legs', and 'outstretched arms in a horizontal line at the level of the shoulders'. Nearly all nritta is performed in araimandi. Key postures land on beats (Mallick et al.). Our classifyMudra and getSpecificMudraScore already score 28 mudras by rule. Hand landmarks from full-body video need the wrist-crop second pass that the deleted /mocap page used.
   - Sources: https://hekint.org/2022/03/25/anatomy-of-the-araimandi/ ; https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.11023 ; https://doaj.org/article/62cab9f5f36b466991e8fb487e74ca74
   - Design implication: Absolute checks, each gated by visibility:
- aramandi depth (knee flexion) and turnout: knees out past the ankles, knee spread at least about 1.5x hip width;
- heels close together;
- hips level;
- torso within about 10° of vertical (from the front, sideways tilt only);
- wrists or elbows within a band of shoulder height in natyarambhe;
- left/right symmetry in symmetric postures;
- foot-strike regularity from ankle vertical velocity or audio onsets.
Check mudras only on the chosen step, and only when the hand crop is large enough (about 80 px or more) and the hand landmarker is confident. Otherwise say 'hands too small to judge'.
17. **[medium] The app can propose steps and say what matters automatically, using motion energy, posture state and self-similarity. For the important within-step weighting, one reference is not enough to learn variability, so a human confirmation step is still needed.**
   - Evidence: Müller & Röder's motion templates mask out the variable parts of a motion class, but they learn this from several examples. Unsupervised segmentation methods use self-similarity or affinity matrices and spectral clustering. Our segment.ts cuts on lower-body stillness (a teacher talking vs dancing), which works for teaching videos but needs the posture gate described above.
   - Sources: https://www.audiolabs-erlangen.com/content/05_fau/professor/00_mueller/03_publications/2006_MuellerRoeder_MotionTemplates_SCA.pdf ; https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/sca.20141135.167-176 ; https://caig.cs.nycu.edu.tw/research/AMR/Lin_AMR_TVCG11_preprint.pdf
   - Design implication: Automatic: dance vs talk from lower-body energy plus aramandi state; steps from the remaining dance runs; repetitions from self-similarity; per-joint importance within a step from reference motion energy plus Bharatanatyam priors (legs and arms high, head and face low for nritta). Human: confirm, rename, drop or merge steps on a timeline. For /learn lessons, use the manifest's steps and cues directly.
